A plan carries the controls that matter for this household. Move one and the figures it feeds recalculate while you watch, so "what if we waited two more years" becomes something you answer in the room rather than a follow-up item.

## What a lever is

A lever is a labeled control wired into the plan's math. Most are sliders with a range, a step and a starting value. Some are a switch with two labeled positions, and some are a short row of option buttons.

| What you want to move | How it usually shows up |
| --- | --- |
| Retirement spending | A slider, for example 110,000 to 190,000 a year in steps of 2,000 |
| What they save each month | A slider from today's figure up to a stretch figure |
| When they claim Social Security | A slider from 62 to 70 in one-year steps, or a switch between claiming early and delaying |
| The return the plan assumes | A slider that shifts returns above or below your firm's assumption, for example one point either way in quarter-point steps |
| Pension against lump sum | A row of option buttons, one per option |

The controls sit together in a bordered card. The card is titled for the question it answers, and it opens on the values the plan was published with. Where Hazel has not named it, the card reads **Optimized plan**.

Most often that card sits on a priority card in the **Your priorities** tab of **Getting there**, beside the recommendation it belongs to. Some plans put it on **Getting there** itself, above the tabs, and some carry a few bare sliders inside one of the tabs instead of a card. So if a plan looks like it has no controls, scan **Getting there** and its tabs before you conclude there are none.

Inside the card, the control with the largest effect on the outcome is listed first, and a short helper line can sit underneath a control to explain what its starting value represents.

One control can appear on two priority cards when it changes both of their numbers. It is the same control in both places, so moving it on one card moves it on the other.

<Note>
  In your firm's Plan design the same card is called **Levers to try**. That is the name to look for when you are shaping a layout, not when you are reading a plan.
</Note>

## What happens when you move one

The figures that control feeds recalculate immediately, in your browser. The success gauge and the probability bars usually lead, along with every other figure and chart Hazel wired to that control. Figures in the other tabs of **Getting there** re-solve too, and switching tabs leaves your positions where you put them.

There are two things a moved control will not do.

- **The prose does not rewrite itself.** The words around a chart, and the figures written into those words, were set when the plan was published. Move a control far enough and a sentence can still quote the earlier number while the gauge beside it shows the new one. If that difference matters, ask Hazel to update the section and it comes back as a new version.
- **Nothing is written down.** A moved control lives in your browser only. Reload the page and every control returns to the value the plan published. Nothing you move can damage the plan.

### Putting a lever back

A card carries one button at the bottom, labeled for what it returns to, and reading **Reset to optimized** where Hazel has not named it. It returns every control in the whole plan to the setting Hazel recommends, not only the one you touched and not only the ones in that card. Reach for it to start clean rather than to undo a single move, because it clears what you were exploring on the other cards and in the other tabs too.

The button wakes up as soon as any control anywhere in the plan sits off its recommended setting. So a live Reset on a card you have not touched means you moved something elsewhere, not something here. Where the sliders sit bare in a tab rather than in a bordered card there is no Reset button at all, and reloading the page is how you put those back.

A new plan arrives with its controls already on Hazel's recommended settings, so there Reset simply undoes your exploration. On a plan where a different position has since been approved and saved, Reset goes to the recommendation rather than to the plan's saved position, so read the values before you present them.

Reset changes only what is on your screen, which is why it works on an older version you are reading as well. Stepping between versions carries a control you moved along with you, unless that version changed the control itself, so reach for Reset when you want an older version read exactly as it was published.

### When a lever moves nothing

Two short notices explain this, and each one is the plan being straight with you, not a fault.

- A line under the controls saying they are preview only means the projections do not respond to them yet. Hazel drew the control but could not wire it into this plan's math. The wording narrows to "not every control here" when only some of them are inert.
- A line under one visual saying it shows the plan as published means that visual is holding the published scenario while the figures around it re-solve. **Range of outcomes** and **Mix comparison** are the two that do this, because their shapes are stored as fixed numbers. The line appears the moment your controls leave their published positions and disappears when they go back.

With no notice at all, a control that moves nothing means the figures genuinely do not depend on it. In all three cases, ask Hazel in chat for the analysis you were after instead of hunting for a control that will produce it.

## Making an explored value stick

Moving a control explores the plan without changing it. To make a set of explored values the plan's position, press **Approve** on the recommendation the card sits with. Approve reads whatever the controls are showing, records that you took the recommendation with your modifications, and writes a row into the plan's **Decisions** section, where it reads either "Approved as suggested" or "Approved with modifications". Then press **Save** in the bar at the bottom of the plan, which commits it as a new version.

If Approve is grayed out and hovering it reads "Requires plan recalculation", the change needs Hazel to re-run the math, so ask for it in chat instead. Controls that do not sit with a recommendation have no Approve either, so those changes are asked for in chat as well and come back as a new version.

<Tip>
  In a client meeting, move the controls freely. Nothing is recorded until you approve and save. When the household lands on a set of values they like, approve the recommendation and save before you leave the meeting, because otherwise the values go with the page.
</Tip>

<Warning>
  Dragging a card into a new order, one of the goal cards for instance, is the one gesture here that is not exploration. Hazel treats an order as a statement, so the drag stages a change straight away: the top bar shows an unsaved change, and you cannot export until you save it or discard it. Where changing the order needs Hazel to re-solve the plan, the grip is inert and hovering it reads "Reordering this plan needs a Hazel round-trip".
</Warning>

<Note>
  A downloaded PDF prints the card as the settled values the plan was published with, under its own heading, or **Plan settings** where Hazel has not named it. It points the reader back to Hazel to explore alternatives. Nothing in a PDF moves.
</Note>

## Adding a lever

Ask for it in the conversation about the plan. Reading the plan from its chat, **Edit** in the top bar opens that conversation. Reading it on the household's **Financial Planning** tab, **Open chat** goes to the same place. Be specific about the range.

```text
Add a retirement-age lever from 60 to 70 in one-year steps, starting
at 62, and show its effect on the success gauge.
```

```text
Add a savings lever so they can see what another 500 a month buys.
```

A vague request works, and you get Hazel's judgment on the range. Naming the range, the step and the starting value gets you the control you pictured.

Some requests come back as analysis instead of a control.

- A control has to be something your browser can recompute on the spot. Amounts, ages, percentages, switches and either/or choices all qualify. A Roth conversion amount does not, because which bracket a conversion fills and what it does to a Medicare premium two years later does not move in a straight line. Hazel settles that in the conversation and writes the answer into the plan.
- Ordering is not something you ask for as a new control. Where a plan ranks cards and the order is one your browser can apply, the grip is already on each card. Where the order feeds a calculation, the grip is there but inert, and you ask Hazel for the reorder in chat.

If you asked for a control and no card appeared, say so in the same chat and ask where it went.

Your firm can also prescribe the sliders a lever card should offer, up to twelve per card. In Plan design, open **Design layout**, select the **Levers to try** card, open **Advanced**, and add rows under **Levers**: a label, a range, and an optional step, starting value and unit. A prescription is a floor rather than a ceiling, so the same sliders tend to arrive on every plan and Hazel may add its own alongside them. See [Block settings](/guides/financial-planning/plan-design/block-settings).

## Levers compared to scenario comparisons

They answer different questions.

|  | Lever | Scenario comparison |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Question** | "How does this figure move if we change one thing?" | "Which of these two or three options is better?" |
| **Form** | One control you move, with the plan re-solving around it | Two or three named options side by side, compared row by row |
| **Best for** | Exploring sensitivity in the room | Presenting a decision the household has to make |
| **How to get one** | Ask for a lever | Ask for a comparison |

Ask for a lever when the thing you are changing has a range and any value in it is possible: what age they retire, what they save each month, what they spend in retirement. An either/or works as a lever too, as a switch or a row of option buttons.

Ask for a comparison when you want both answers on the screen at once. A comparison holds two or three columns, marks the better option row by row with a small **Better** badge, and can carry one closing line underneath. Its columns re-solve with your controls too, so moving a slider moves the comparison as well.

```text
Compare taking the pension as a monthly benefit against the lump sum,
side by side, mark the better option on each row, and add a closing
line saying which you would take.
```
